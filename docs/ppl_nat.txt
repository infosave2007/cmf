The history of astronomy is the story of how people learned to read the sky. For thousands of years, farmers and sailors watched the sun, the moon, and the stars to know when to plant their crops and how to find their way across the sea. The ancient Egyptians noticed that the brightest star in the sky appeared just before the yearly flood of the Nile, and they built their calendar around it. In Babylon, priests kept careful records of the planets for centuries, writing their observations on clay tablets that survive to this day.
The Greeks were the first to ask not only where the stars were, but why they moved as they did. Aristotle argued that the Earth stood still at the center of the universe, and for nearly two thousand years most people believed him. It was not until the sixteenth century that Nicolaus Copernicus placed the sun at the center instead, and the old picture of the heavens began to fall apart. Galileo pointed his small telescope at Jupiter and saw four moons circling another world, a simple observation that changed everything.
Isaac Newton showed that the same force that pulls an apple to the ground also holds the moon in its orbit. His law of gravity explained the motion of the planets with a single idea, and it guided astronomers for more than two centuries. When telescopes grew larger, the universe grew with them. William Herschel discovered Uranus in 1781, the first new planet found since ancient times, and later he mapped the shape of our galaxy by counting stars in every direction.
In the twentieth century, Edwin Hubble proved that many of the faint spirals in the night sky were entire galaxies far beyond our own, each containing billions of stars. He also found that these galaxies were moving away from us, and that the farther they were, the faster they receded. This discovery led to the idea of an expanding universe and, eventually, to the theory of the Big Bang. Today, space telescopes watch the sky in light our eyes cannot see, and astronomers study planets around other stars, some of which may hold liquid water. The questions have grown larger, but the impulse is the same one the first sky-watchers felt: to look up and wonder.
